About

I'm a graduate research assistant at Carnegie Mellon working on the Argus CubeSat mission—specifically payload development and image recognition for autonomous navigation. The core problem: how do you orient a satellite in orbit when GPS isn't an option? We're teaching it to recognize where it is by what it sees.
Beyond satellites I am working on machine learning—specifically the parts where statistical rigor meets the messiness of real-world reasoning. I care about building systems that are not just accurate but trustworthy, and understanding why they work when they do.

Currently at CMU. Fueled by coffee and a persistent belief that theory and practice should talk to each other more.

Research

I think about how to make ML systems that can reason and not just pattern-match—and how to do so responsibly. This means working at the intersection of neural methods, symbolic structures, and statistical foundations.

Current obsessions: How do we get the best of neural flexibility and symbolic precision? What does "fairness" even mean when your model is a black box? Can we build systems that know what they don't know? Can we blend epistemology and ontology in neural models ?
If you have thoughts on this, I'd love to hear from you.
Current work: Image recognition for spacecraft attitude determination (the "lost in space" problem). How do you figure out where you're pointing when you can't rely on GPS? Computer vision, star tracking, and a lot of orbital mechanics
